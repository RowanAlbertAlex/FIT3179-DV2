Beyond the Median

Australia’s median household earned $1,746 a week in 2021. But that single number hides a much wider distribution of household incomes across the country.

What does Australia’s income distribution actually look like?

Before looking at where income differs across Australia, start with the national picture.

Nearly one-quarter (24.3%) of households with stated income reported earning $3,000+ per week in 2021. But this national figure hides important differences between places.

Scale and income share tell different stories.

Larger cities naturally have more households and often more high-income households in absolute terms. That does not necessarily mean high-income households form a larger share of the city.

Sydney contains about 552,000 households earning $3,000+ per week, around 32% of households reporting income. Canberra has far fewer of these households — about 61,000 — but the highest share of the capital-city regions shown, at about 38%.

Reading both channels together separates scale from prevalence: a large circle means many higher-income households, while the darker colour indicates that they form a larger share of the city.

City size does not automatically produce the strongest high-income profile. Sydney stands out for the number of higher-income households; Canberra for how common they are within the city.

But did higher incomes mean greater purchasing power?

Between 2011 and 2021, the share of families reporting $3,000+ weekly income increased. But these are nominal incomes: higher dollar amounts do not necessarily mean greater purchasing power.

Real equivalised disposable income adjusts for tax, inflation and household size, unlike Census family income. Its weekly median barely moved: $1,455 (2021) to $1,464 (2024).

The median barely moved — but income groups did

Gold shows 2021; teal shows 2024 real weekly income.
Centre labels identify each household fifth and inflation-adjusted change.

A recovery for some. A decline for others.

The lowest two income groups experienced small gains in real income. But the middle and higher-income groups remained below their 2021 levels.

Housing circumstances also mattered. From 2021 to 2024, households with a mortgage experienced a 7.7% decline in real income, compared with a 9.7% increase for owners without a mortgage and 5.5% for renters.

These are national estimates, not changes tracked for individual households. They show why the same economic period could produce different experiences.

Beyond the
median.

A national median provides a starting point, not a complete picture. Australia's income distribution differs across places, family types and income groups. A large number of high-income households does not always mean high incomes are common, and rising dollar incomes do not necessarily mean stronger purchasing power.

Understanding household income means looking beyond a single figure — at who earns it, where they live and what that income can buy.
